In 1971, Hans Haacke compiled public records relating to 142 buildings in Manhattan owned by companies belonging to a single family and presented them in the form of photographs and typewritten sheets. It was not secret information, but the mere fact that the information was made legible by showing the connections between the entries was enough for the Guggenheim to cancel the exhibition.

## Capta

Someone always decides what to record, how to name it and how to present it. Johanna Drucker suggests referring to data as [_capta_](http://www.digitalhumanities.org/dhq/vol/5/1/000091/000091.html), from the Latin for ‘taken’, rather than ‘data’. However, as Catherine D’Ignazio observes, we accept [charts as facts](https://civic.mit.edu/index.html%3Fp=2165.html) because they appear scientific, general and neutral. Geoffrey Bowker and Susan Leigh Star demonstrate the same principle in relation to classifications ([_Sorting Things Out_](https://attachments.are.na/49371746/f0556d23ff5e875693bb1a2fdcd1f448.pdf)): each makes certain lives visible whilst rendering others invisible. D’Ignazio and Lauren Klein pose three questions for anyone working with data ([_Data Feminism_](https://attachments.are.na/46622501/5fdd11ff6d2ae5f54b41d42007e57271.pdf)): by whom, for whom and in whose interest?

## Pointing

Charles Sanders Peirce divided signs into three categories. An icon resembles what it depicts; a symbol represents it by convention; an index is linked to it. Smoke indicates fire; a footprint indicates that someone has passed that way. Building on this, Dietmar Offenhuber and Orkan Telhan examine [indexical visualisation](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315781129-31): representations that do not translate data into a new form but refer to the thing itself. [_Cloud Studies_](https://forensic-architecture.org/investigation/cloudstudies) (2020) by Forensic Architecture interprets clouds of toxic smoke as evidence.

## Evidence

Investigators use videos, photos and audio recordings posted online by strangers to show where and how the violence took place. Eyal Weizman and Matthew Fuller define this approach as [investigative aesthetics](https://attachments.are.na/43821297/84e2b77272ed574edb3e2889ce6d4cb2.pdf): making something perceptible so that it can be challenged. The result often appears deliberately raw, as if this very rawness were to guarantee its veracity.
