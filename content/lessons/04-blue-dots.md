On a phone map you are a blue dot in the middle. For Joost Grootens this is one of the biggest changes in mapmaking: maps used to be made by specialists and read by others, now the map is cropped around you and partly made by you. [Simon Weckert](https://www.simonweckert.com/googlemapshacks.html) pulled 99 phones in a cart through an empty Berlin street, and Google Maps reported a traffic jam. The map is watching you back. [*The New York Times*](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2018/12/10/business/location-data-privacy-apps.html) traced "anonymous" location data to the people who produced it. So much for anonymous.
Against this mirror Grootens proposes the *blind map*, a map without names that you complete yourself.

## Counting

A map shows what someone decided to count. [Suzanne Lacy](https://www.suzannelacy.com/three-weeks-in-may) stamped every rape reported in Los Angeles onto a public map. On [Wheelmap](https://wheelmap.org/), wheelchair users mark which places they can enter. Even a border is a decision, and on Google Maps it moves [depending on who's looking](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2020/02/14/google-maps-political-borders/): seen from India, Kashmir is entirely Indian; from everywhere else, it is crossed by dotted lines. Google says it follows local law.

## Distance

How far away we stand decides what we see. [*Powers of Ten*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0fKBhvDjuy0) goes from a picnic to the galaxy in a few minutes. [Jenny Odell](https://www.jennyodell.com/satellite.html) cuts single objects out of satellite views until we see them again. Hito Steyerl, in ["In Free Fall"](https://www.e-flux.com/journal/24/67860/in-free-fall-a-thought-experiment-on-vertical-perspective/), asks who gets to look down.

<br>

_In short:_ The map is cropped around you, and it shows only what someone chose to count.
