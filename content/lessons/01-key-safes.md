# Key Safes

> Interface is not a thing, but a zone of affordances

In 2026 [Women on Waves](https://www.womenonwaves.org/en/page/7875/abortion-key-safes-in-malta) hid key safes around Malta. Women who are less than nine weeks pregnant can send an email to the collective and receive a place and a code to access them. This is an example of an interface built where official interfaces fail. [Alarm Phone](https://alarmphone.org) is a phone number for people in distress at sea, in cases where the coast guard can't be trusted to rescue them. It alerts the authorities and documents what's happening. [Pirate Care](https://syllabus.pirate.care/) collects practices like these, which take _care_ out of the institutions that restrict it. [Open Insulin](https://openinsulin.org/) and [Open Rampette](https://rampette.opencare.cc/) are examples of interfaces based on instructions instead of products.

## Window or mirror

A key safe works because most people walk past it. It is invisible to the authorities and obvious to whoever holds the code: its invisibility is a design decision about who is excluded. Most digital interfaces also want to disappear, but for everyone, including the people using them. Can they?
Jay David Bolter and Diane Gromala call this the myth of transparency ([_Windows and Mirrors_](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262524490/windows-and-mirrors/), 2003): every window is also a mirror, showing us our actions and our place in the system. For [Alexander Galloway](https://attachments.are.na/963061/8e0eb86eca872c2388940db6d787c0ac.pdf?1491480957) (2012), "an interface is not a thing, an interface is always an effect. It is always a process or a translation." For [Johanna Drucker](https://attachments.are.na/23491749/923ae476dd101951b2b630d633621fea.pdf) (2011), "an interface is not so much a 'between' space as it is the mediating environment that makes the experience, a 'critical zone that constitutes a user experience'."

## Learning the interface

An interface only looks transparent once we have learned it. Before it was a screen, it was imagined as a way to share what we know: in 1945 Vannevar Bush described the [memex](https://archive.org/details/as-we-may-think), a desk where a researcher could record trails through documents and pass them on. Starting in 1960, [Ted Nelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Xanadu) worked on Xanadu, a system meant to keep every quote connected to where it came from. When computers arrived at home, all of this had to be taught. Magazines like [_Byte_](https://archive.org/details/byte-magazine-1982-01) explained it to hobbyists, and in 1995 the CBC still had to show its viewers [how to use the internet](https://youtube.com/watch?v=Y0EXga2hEIs). Every guide also imagined a user. Like the key safe, an interface is obvious to some and invisible to others. Which interfaces did you have to learn, and who taught you?

<br>

_In short:_ An interface is a relation between people, and it doesn't need a screen.
