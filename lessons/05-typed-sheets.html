Typed Sheets

Pointing towards what?

Data have no necessary visual form.

Alexander R. Galloway, ‘Are Some Things Unrepresentable?’, 2011

In 1971, Hans Haacke compiled public records relating to 142 buildings in Manhattan owned by companies belonging to a single family and presented them in the form of photographs and typewritten sheets. It was not secret information, but the mere fact that the information was made legible by showing the connections between the entries was enough for the Guggenheim to cancel the exhibition.

Capta

Someone always decides what to record, how to name it and how to present it. Johanna Drucker suggests referring to data as capta, from the Latin for ‘taken’, rather than ‘data’. However, as Catherine D’Ignazio observes, we accept charts as facts because they appear scientific, general and neutral. Geoffrey Bowker and Susan Leigh Star demonstrate the same principle in relation to classifications (Sorting Things Out): each makes certain lives visible whilst rendering others invisible. D’Ignazio and Lauren Klein pose three questions for anyone working with data (Data Feminism): by whom, for whom and in whose interest?

Conventions

To represent data, someone must give it a form. In Sémiologie graphique (1967), Jacques Bertin listed the variables that a sign can utilise: its position on the page, size, value, texture, colour, orientation and shape. Every graph is constructed from these elements.
Galloway insists that data do not have a pre-established visual form; every visualisation must invent its own rules for transforming numbers into signs. So it is ‘first and foremost a visualization of the conversion rules themselves’, and only then of the data. His essay begins with an image taken from the New York Times in 2010, showing a PowerPoint presentation on US strategy in Afghanistan. It is a network diagram featuring dozens of arrows and colours, accompanied by General McChrystal’s comment: ‘When we understand that slide, we’ll have won the war’.

Mediation is missing. There is, in a very literal sense, no media happening here.

Pointing

Charles Sanders Peirce divided signs into three categories. An icon resembles what it depicts; a symbol represents it by convention; an index is linked to it. Smoke indicates fire; a footprint indicates that someone has passed that way. Building on this, Dietmar Offenhuber and Orkan Telhan examine indexical visualisation: representations that do not translate data into a new form but refer to the thing itself. Cloud Studies (2020) by Forensic Architecture interprets clouds of toxic smoke as evidence.

Evidence

Investigators use videos, photos and audio recordings posted online by strangers to show where and how the violence took place. Eyal Weizman and Matthew Fuller define this approach as investigative aesthetics: making something perceptible so that it can be challenged. The result often appears deliberately raw, as if this very rawness were to guarantee its veracity.


TLDR: Data is taken, not given, and it can be made to answer back to power.
Reading: Eyal Weizman, Matthew Fuller, Investigative Aesthetics, 2021
