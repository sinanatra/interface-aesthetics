Key Safes

What is an interface?

Interface is not a thing, but a zone of affordances

Johanna Drucker, ‘Humanities Approaches to Interface Theory’, 2011

In 2026 Women on Waves hid key safes at various locations across Malta. Women who are less than nine weeks pregnant can send an email to the collective and receive details of a location and a code to access it. Alarm Phoneis a helpline for people in distress at sea, in cases where the coastguard cannot be trusted to rescue them. It alerts the authorities and documents what is happening. Pirate Care brings together initiatives such as these, which take care out of the hands of institutions that restrict it.

Window or mirror

A key safe works because most people walk past it without noticing it. Its invisibility is a design choice that affects those who are excluded. Most digital interfaces also aim to disappear, but in the eyes of everyone, including the people who use them. But can they really do that?
Jay David Bolter and Diane Gromala define this phenomenon as the myth of transparency (Windows and Mirrors, 2003): every window is also a mirror, showing us our actions and our place within the system. For Alexander Galloway (2012), ‘an interface is not a thing; an interface is always an effect. It is always a process or a translation’. According to Johanna Drucker (2011), an “intermediate” space as the mediating environment that creates the experience, a “critical zone that constitutes the user’s experience”’.

The shape of the interface

In 1945, Vannevar Bush described the memex, a desk where a researcher could record paths through documents and transmit them. This concept has its roots in the seventeenth century, when the Jesuit Athanasius Kircher devised a physical interface for musical composition. From 1960 onwards, Ted Nelson worked on Xanadu, a system designed to keep every citation linked to its source.
When computers then entered our homes, these new ways of interacting had to be explained to the general public: magazines like Byte explained them to enthusiasts, and as late as 1995, the CBC was showing its viewers how to use the internet.What interfaces did you imagine, and which ones did you have to learn? Who taught them to you?


TLDR: An interface is a relation between people, and it doesn’t need a screen.
Reading: Jay David Bolter, Diane Gromala, Windows and Mirrors, 2003
