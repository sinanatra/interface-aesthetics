Answers

Why make something that can be generated in seconds?

The visual is too loaded to be of any use.

Vinzenz Aubry, ‘In Defense of No Image’, 2024

In May 2024, Google began responding to searches autonomously, with an ‘AI Overview’ appearing at the top of the results. When asked how to stop cheese from sliding off a pizza, it suggested adding glue to the sauce. The origin of this advice was a joke posted on Reddit in 2012. Google’s response was itself a joke, but it had lost its meaning, which depended on the context.

No source to question

Until a few years ago, we would carry out a search and get a list of results. Now we type a prompt and get a single answer. Umberto Eco, in The Infinity of Lists (2009), regards the list as a way of knowing that never claims to be complete. A results page shows that there is more to come and leaves the choice (partly) up to us.

No images

With image generators, observes Vinzenz Aubry in ‘In Defense of No Image’ (2024), the relationship is reversed: the prompt, the description, refers to the image, not the other way round. His title echoes Hito Steyerl’s ‘In Defense of the Poor Image’ (2009), which analysed the media significance of low-resolution images. For Aubry, however, an image in itself represents nothing. A generated image has no meaning, unlike the sentence that generated it. This is the death of representation. If an image takes a second to generate, the question is no longer how to create it, but why. We can take this discussion further by asking ourselves for whom we should be creating interfaces. For humans, or for bots that can read them? From Parrika’s operational images to ‘operational interfaces’.


TLDR: A generated answer drops its sources, a generated image points to nothing.
Reading: Vinzenz Aubry, ‘In Defense of No Image’, 2024
