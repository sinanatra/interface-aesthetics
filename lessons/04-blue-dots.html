Blue Dots

What does an interface make perceptible, and to whom?

Producing and using are not consecutive processes but parallel tracks.

Joost Grootens, Blind Maps and Blue Dots, 2020

On Google Maps, you’re a blue dot in the centre.
Joost Grootens wrote his doctoral thesis on this very topic, viewing it as one of the most significant changes in the history of cartography. Maps used to be drawn by specialists and read by other experts. Now, however, the map is tailored to us, created by us and read by us. Simon Weckert pulled 99 mobile phones on a trolley along a deserted street in Berlin, and Google Maps reported a traffic jam. The New York Times analysed ‘anonymous’ location data, revealing just how little ‘anonymous’ it actually was. Faced with this reality, Grootens proposes the blind map, a map without names that you complete yourself.
A map always shows what someone has decided to count. Suzanne Lacy, in 1977, marked every reported rape in Los Angeles on a map. Wheelmap shows places accessible by wheelchair. Even borders are a matter of choice, and on Google Maps they shift depending on who’s looking at them: seen from India, Kashmir is entirely Indian; from every other place, it is criss-crossed by dotted lines.
Zooming changes the way we see reality. Powers of Ten moves from a picnic to the galaxy in a matter of minutes. Jenny Odell cuts out individual objects from satellite images and collages them. Hito Steyerl, in ‘In Free Fall’, asks who has the right to look down from above.


TLDR: The map is cropped around you, and it shows only what someone chose to count.
Reading: Joost Grootens, Blind Maps and Blue Dots, 2020
